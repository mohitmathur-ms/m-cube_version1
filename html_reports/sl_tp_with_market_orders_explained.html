§1 — The short answer

Your trade opens the instant the signal fires — that part is a market order and it is correct. The stop-loss and take-profit are not separate orders sitting at the broker. Instead, the program remembers two prices ("close me out if price falls to X" and "close me out if price rises to Y") and on every single bar it asks "did this bar touch X or Y?". The moment a bar does, it sells out with another market order. So the trade really does exit only when SL or target is reached — m-cube just enforces that itself, bar by bar, instead of leaving a resting order on the exchange.
Technically: SL/TP are synthetic. On entry fill, ManagedExitStrategy computes self.current_sl and self.current_tp as plain float attributes (managed_strategy.py:1592–1639). Each on_bar compares the bar's high/low against those floats (1220–1297) and, on a breach, calls close_all_positions(...) — a market close. No stop_market, limit, or bracket order is ever submitted in the production strategies; the backtest engine never holds a resting protective order.

§2 — The entry path (instant market order)

When a strategy decides to go long or short, it places a "buy/sell at whatever the market is right now" order. In a backtest that fills on the current bar — instantly. That is expected and fine; the entry is not where SL/TP belongs.
Technically: _submit_order(side) builds a market order with TimeInForce.GTC and submits it. The optional tags=[reason] carries the entry-reason string through to fills_report["tags"] for the orderbook view. Every production strategy (EMA Cross, RSI, Bollinger, Four MA, Range Breakout) routes through this exact market path.

§3 — Where SL/TP are stored

The moment the entry fills, the program calculates the two exit prices and saves them. It does not send them anywhere — they are just two numbers held in memory for as long as the trade is open.
Technically: in the order-filled handler the engine sets self.current_sl and self.current_tp based on the configured type — percentage, points, atr, or trailing — snapping each to the instrument tick. A type of 0.0 means "no SL/TP of that kind", which disables the corresponding check in §4.

§4 — Where the SL/TP logic actually lives (the core answer)

This is the part you were looking for. On every bar, before deciding anything, the engine first updates the stop (for trailing / move-to-cost / profit-lock), then asks two questions: "did the bar dip to my stop?" and "did the bar reach my target?". If yes, it exits. There is no broker order doing this — it is a simple comparison running once per bar inside on_bar.
Technically: the SL test is intrabar — a long leg's SL fires when bar_low <= current_sl; a short leg's when bar_high >= current_sl. The TP test is mirrored (long fires on bar_high >= current_tp). Both support an optional confirmation delay: sl_wait_sec/tgt_wait_sec (wall-clock) or the legacy sl_wait_bars/tgt_wait_bars (bar count). Which price series feeds bar_high/bar_low is decided by resolve_trigger_hl(...) (managed_strategy.py:152–173): Format A bid/ask (long SL on ask-low, short SL on bid-high), Format B the slot's own OHLCV high/low, Format C LTP (high and low both collapse to close).

§5 — How the exit fires

When a stop or target is hit, the position is closed with another market order — and depending on your configuration the strategy can then do nothing, try again, or flip to the opposite direction.
Technically: _handle_exit(...) → _close_with_reason(reason) → self.close_all_positions(instrument_id, tags=[reason]) (managed_strategy.py:1020–1034). The reason tag (e.g. Stop Loss, Target) propagates into the fills/orderbook view. The post-exit action is dispatched from on_sl_action / on_target_action: close (no-op, already flat), re_execute (re-arm after a delay), reverse (submit a fresh market order on the opposite side and recompute SL/TP), execute (arm a sibling slot via the cross-slot bus), or re_entry (price-wait re-entry trigger).

§6 — "Take a trade only when SL/target hits" — clearing up the confusion

Your phrasing mixes two different ideas. One is "I want my trade to exit only when the stop or target is reached" — that already happens (§4). The other is "I want my trade to enter only after price comes to a level I chose" — that is a pending/limit entry order, which m-cube does not do today. It's worth being clear about which one you mean, because the answer is completely different.
Technically: the instant market fill governs when you get in, not when you get out. SL/TP are exit logic and are fully wired. A "wait for price, then enter" behavior would require a resting limit / stop_market entry order (or a price-gate before _submit_order) — see §7.

§7 — Should we use Nautilus limit orders instead?

Limit/stop orders aren't a strict upgrade — they trade one set of assumptions for another. The synthetic approach is simpler and supports clever trailing logic; exchange-native orders model fills more realistically but are harder to combine with trailing/move-to-cost. Pick based on what you're trying to measure.
Technically: NautilusTrader can express all three patterns. A working bracket/OCO example already exists in-repo at custom_strategies/custom_practice_strat3.py:135–178 — a MARKET entry with a STOP_MARKET SL child and a MARKET_IF_TOUCHED TP child under ContingencyType.OUO, submitted via submit_order_list(...). The cost of moving to exchange-native orders is that the trailing-SL, move-to-cost, target-lock, and SL/TP-wait features built into ManagedExitStrategy would need to re-issue/modify orders each bar instead of mutating a float.

Recommendation.
  • Keep synthetic SL/TP as the default. It is deterministic, fast, and is the only approach that cleanly supports the trailing / move-to-cost / profit-lock / SL-&-target-wait logic already wired into ManagedExitStrategy. For research backtesting this is the right tool.
  • Reach for bracket/OCO orders only when you specifically need realistic fill behavior (slippage, gap-throughs, partial fills) or want to mirror how live trading would behave — this belongs in the future L4 execution layer / Path B, not the per-bar research path.
  • Use limit entry orders only if the strategy's edge is genuinely about waiting for price to come to you. That changes entry semantics (the order may never fill) and needs deliberate fill modeling — don't adopt it just to "make SL/TP work", because SL/TP already work.

§8 — Caveats of the synthetic approach

The bar-by-bar method is convenient but slightly optimistic. It assumes you always get filled at exactly your stop/target price the moment a bar touches it. Real markets gap and slip, so live results can be a bit worse than the backtest implies — keep that in mind when reading P&L.
Watch out for:
  • Optimistic fills: a touch of current_sl/current_tp is assumed to fill at that exact price with no slippage. A real stop can gap straight through and fill worse.
  • SL-before-TP ordering: within one bar the SL check runs before the TP check (§4). If a single bar's range spans both levels, the SL wins — the engine cannot know the true intrabar path.
  • Format C (LTP): when the trigger series is last-price only, high and low both collapse to close, so wicks that would have hit a level are invisible.
  • Granularity: coarser bars (e.g. 1-day) make intrabar touches less precise; finer bars give more faithful SL/TP timing.
