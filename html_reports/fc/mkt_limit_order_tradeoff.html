1TL;DR verdict

Recommendation

For your bar-based backtests and your momentum entries (EMA cross, four-MA, range breakout), MARKET is the safer, more conventional, and more honest default. Keep it.

The reason isn't that LIMIT orders are bad — in live trading they're a legitimate cost-saving tool. The reason is specific to how you backtest: m-cube feeds the engine OHLCV bars, not ticks. A MARKET order has one obvious, deterministic fill. A LIMIT order's fill is a guess — the simulator has to decide whether your limit "would have" filled inside a bar it can only see as four numbers. That guess is almost always optimistic, and optimistic fills quietly inflate backtest results.

LIMIT is worth it only for mean-reversion entries (RSI, Bollinger — buying a dip at a better price) and only if you model the fill assumption honestly. Otherwise you're trading a real, measurable cost (the spread, which you already model) for a fake, invisible one (overstated fill rates).

2How m-cube fills orders today

Every entry in the platform is a MARKET order, time-in-force GTC. That is true in the exit-management engine and in every bundled strategy:

  • core/managed_strategy.py:1765 — order = self.order_factory.market(...), with TimeInForce.GTC set at :1761.
  • Same pattern in strategies/ema_cross.py:105, bollinger_bands.py:97, four_ma.py:101, rsi_mean_reversion.py:95, range_breakout.py:458, and the custom-strategy template at core/custom_strategy_loader.py:459.
  • Exits (SL / TP / squareoff / reverse) don't even go through the order factory — they call close_all_positions() via _close_with_reason() at core/managed_strategy.py:1480. That's also a market-style flatten.
  • Only GTC is used anywhere. No IOC, FOK, or GTD appears in the codebase.

You already pay for realism — without LIMIT orders

A common reason people reach for LIMIT orders is "to avoid paying the spread." But m-cube already models execution cost on the fill side, independent of order type:

  • BID/ASK auto-pairing (core/backtest_runner.py:2209): each user bar type is paired with its ASK/BID series so the matching engine fills at real spread prices. If that data is missing you get an explicit warning that "fills will use MID prices — spread cost is not reflected" (:2323).
  • VWAP fill model (_USE_VWAP_FILL, :2819) — SL/Target leg exits fill against a session-cumulative volume-weighted price.
  • Directional close fill (_USE_DIRECTIONAL_FILL, :2829) — models the half-spread paid on exit (long exits fill on BID, short exits on ASK).
  • Three-format exit-trigger engine (exit_price_format: ohlcv / ltp / bidask) at core/models/exit_config/__init__.py:29 and core/managed_strategy.py:186.
Key point

The spread cost you'd hope to "save" with a LIMIT order is already represented in your fills. So the LIMIT question isn't "do I want to model the spread?" — you do that already. It's "do I want to gamble on uncertain fills to not pay it?"

3The core problem: bar data makes LIMIT fills ambiguous

This is the single most important section. A MARKET order and a LIMIT order behave completely differently inside a bar-driven simulator.

MARKET order on a bar

Submitted on bar N → fills deterministically (at that bar's close, or next bar's open, per the engine's rule). One price. No ambiguity. The simulation and a real broker would agree on "yes, you got in."

LIMIT order on a bar

Rests on the book. The simulator fills it if the bar's range touches the price — typically low ≤ limit ≤ high. But OHLC tells you the bar reached that price; it does not tell you the intrabar path, the time spent there, or whether you'd have been at the front of the queue.

Why "touched the price" ≠ "would have filled"

  • Queue position is unknowable. Price may have ticked your level for a fraction of a second with thin volume — real fills depend on who was ahead of you. Bar data has none of this.
  • Adverse selection. In live markets a resting limit tends to fill precisely when the market is about to move against you (you bought right before it kept falling). A naive bar simulator fills you and ignores that the move continued.
  • It's an optimistic bias, and it's invisible. Every "touched-the-price" fill the simulator grants you that you wouldn't really have gotten makes the backtest look better than reality. Unlike the spread (a known, modeled cost), this error has no line item — it just silently inflates the equity curve.
The trap

Switching entries to LIMIT and seeing the backtest improve is exactly the symptom to distrust. Part of that gain is real spread savings; part is fills you'd never have received. Bar data can't separate the two, so you can't trust the number.

MARKET orders sidestep all of this: their fill is determined, not assumed, so the backtest stays honest by construction.

The pattern is clear: LIMIT wins on cost and slippage control (live-trading concerns), MARKET wins on certainty, fidelity, and simplicity (backtest concerns). Since you're optimizing a backtest first, the table tilts toward MARKET.

5"Faster" means two different things

You asked whether LIMIT would be faster. There are two completely separate notions of speed here, and they point opposite ways.

(a) Backtest simulation speed

  • MARKET: fills immediately on the bar; nothing rests on the book; the matching engine has fewer open orders to re-evaluate each bar.
  • LIMIT: creates a resting order the matching engine must re-check against every subsequent bar until it fills, expires, or is cancelled — plus the cancel/replace traffic you'd add to avoid stale orders.

So in the simulator, MARKET is marginally faster, not slower. But the difference is small and almost never the bottleneck — your real costs are bar loading and the engine run (you already profile these with _PROFILE_PHASES=1). Speed is not a good reason to choose either way for backtesting.

(b) Live execution latency

  • MARKET: executes essentially immediately — you are in the moment the order hits the venue.
  • LIMIT: "faster" is the wrong word — it executes whenever the market comes to your price, if ever. It can be instant (marketable limit) or never.
Don't conflate them

If "faster" means "the backtest runs quicker," MARKET already wins. If it means "I'm in the trade sooner," MARKET wins decisively for momentum. LIMIT is never the faster choice for getting filled — that's the price you pay for a better fill price.

6Which order fits which strategy

Your bundled strategies split into two camps, and the right answer differs between them.

Momentum / breakout → MARKET

EMA Cross, Four MA, Range Breakout. These fire because price is moving in your direction. A LIMIT placed below the market (for a long) sits there while price runs away — you systematically miss your best trades and keep only the ones where the move stalled. That's a selection bias that hurts both the backtest and live.

Mean-reversion → LIMIT is defensible

RSI Mean Reversion, Bollinger Bands. These bet price snaps back from an extreme. Resting a LIMIT at a better price is congruent with the thesis — you wanted to buy lower anyway. Here LIMIT can genuinely cut cost. But the bar-data fill ambiguity from §3 still applies; you must model fills conservatively or you'll overstate it.

Breakout strategies are a special case: the conventional tool there is a STOP / stop-limit order placed above the range (triggers as price breaks out), not a passive limit below the market. See §8.

8Middle-ground options (if you want some of LIMIT's benefits)

  • Keep MARKET, trust your fill models. The cheapest, most honest path: you already model spread via BID/ASK pairing, VWAP, and directional-close fills. This gives you realistic cost without the fill-ambiguity gamble. This is the default recommendation.
  • Marketable limit (limit with a worst-price cap). Place a LIMIT a few pips through the market so it fills like a market order but caps catastrophic slippage. Fills are still near-deterministic in backtest, so fidelity stays high. Good live-trading hardening with low backtest risk.
  • STOP / stop-limit for breakouts. For Range Breakout specifically, a stop entry above the range is the conventional, signal-aligned tool — and the strategy already approximates this by modeling the fill at max(trigger, bar.open) (strategies/range_breakout.py).
  • Time-in-force discipline. If you do use resting limits, don't leave them GTC forever. GTD (expire end of bar/session) or IOC (fill now or cancel) make the backtest assumption explicit and prevent stale fills days later. None of these TIFs are wired today.
  • Passive limit only for mean-reversion, with conservative fills. If you add LIMIT entries for RSI/Bollinger, model the fill pessimistically (e.g. require the bar to trade through the limit, not just touch it) so you don't bank fills you wouldn't get.

9If you did want LIMIT support: where it plugs in

This is a sketch for orientation only — no code is changed by this report. Adding an opt-in LIMIT entry would touch three layers:

1. Schema — add the knobs (core/models/exit_config/__init__.py)

Add fields to ExitConfig (or StrategySlotConfig) so it's per-slot and opt-in, defaulting to today's behaviour:

entry_order_type: str = "market"   # "market" | "limit" | "marketable_limit"
entry_limit_offset: float = 0.0    # ticks/points from signal price (sign by side)
entry_tif: str = "gtc"             # "gtc" | "gtd" | "ioc"

Defaults preserve current results exactly — anything not set stays MARKET/GTC.

2. Serialization — carry them through (core/models/serialization/__init__.py)

Nothing to do beyond adding the fields: _filter_known_fields(exit_data, ExitConfig) already tolerates new keys in both directions, so old portfolio JSON loads with the defaults and new JSON round-trips automatically.

3. Submission — branch in _submit_order (core/managed_strategy.py:1752)

Where it builds the market order today, branch on the new field:

4. The part that matters most — honest fills

Don't skip this

A resting limit will be filled by the matching engine on the optimistic low ≤ limit ≤ high rule (§3). To keep the backtest trustworthy you must either (a) restrict LIMIT entries to mean-reversion slots, (b) require a trade-through rather than a touch, and/or (c) attach a fill-probability/queue model. Wiring the order type without addressing the fill assumption is how a backtest quietly starts lying.

Per repo convention, this should also be gated/flagged like the other execution-realism features (_USE_VWAP_FILL, _USE_DIRECTIONAL_FILL) and reflected in LOGICS_BACKEND_STATUS.md with the appropriate pf-ui-only handling until fully wired.

10Bottom line

Verdict
  • Keep MARKET as the default. For your bar-based backtests it's safer (no fill gaming), more honest, simpler, and faster to both fill and simulate.
  • You're not "missing out" on spread savings. Your fill models already represent spread cost — LIMIT wouldn't add realism, it would add an optimistic-fill risk.
  • Consider LIMIT only for mean-reversion slots (RSI, Bollinger), and only with a conservative fill assumption. For momentum/breakout, a passive limit actively hurts by missing the move.
  • If you want live-trading slippage protection, a marketable limit is the low-risk upgrade — it caps the worst price while staying near-deterministic in backtest.

The headline: LIMIT vs MARKET is not really a "which is better" question — it's a tradeoff between fill certainty and fill price. A backtest, especially a bar-based one, prizes certainty. Live trading prizes price. You're tuning a backtest, so certainty wins — keep MARKET, and revisit LIMIT (carefully) only if and when you take a specific mean-reversion strategy toward live execution.
